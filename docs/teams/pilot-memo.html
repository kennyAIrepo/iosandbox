Research memo · 16 Sep 2026 · Microsoft Teams pilot · hopeOS browser stack

Track locally, ship landmarks, never track the video call

Feeding Teams video back into our tracker is the slow path by construction. The prototype that will feel fast in a demo runs the tracker on each person's own webcam in their own browser, sends only skeletal data between browsers, and hands Teams a finished picture. This memo lays out why, which Teams entry points exist for that, and the small local tests to run before we build.

The problem. A video call already spends roughly 200 to 400 ms getting a frame from one camera to another screen. Screen-capturing that tile and tracking it adds two more hops on top of a compressed, resized picture. Nothing in our tracker can claw that back.

The design. Each participant's browser tracks their own camera (the path we already have), broadcasts a ~600 byte landmark packet at 30 Hz, and renders everyone's avatars in the same scene. The user's own avatar responds within one or two frames. Teams supplies identity, audio, and the meeting container.

Into Teams. Three ways to put the rendered scene in front of Teams users, in order of effort: a virtual camera (works today), a Teams video-effect app (frames processed inside the Teams client, still beta), or our page joining the meeting as a participant through Azure Communication Services.

A · Tapping the Teams video Avoid for avatars

Keep this path for exactly one thing: showing that we can track a person who is not running our app, for example the one colleague who joined from a phone. It is the "after" half of the side-by-side latency demo in T1, and that is its main value. It should never drive anyone's own avatar.

B · Local tracking, landmark relay Core

This is the architecture every avatar-telepresence product uses, including the Mesh 3D spaces Microsoft just retired: capture and inference stay on the participant's device, the network carries pose. Our stack already does the capture and inference half on the user's webcam; what is new is a packet format, a relay, and remote-avatar interpolation.

Three relay choices, to be measured in T2 and T3. A plain WebSocket relay (a 40-line Node script locally, Azure Web PubSub when it has to be Microsoft-hosted: 1 MB per frame, about 1,000 connections per unit). WebRTC data channels, peer-to-peer, unordered and unreliable, lowest latency up to roughly six people, then a small SFU. Live Share, which is the Teams-native choice and free, but every event passes through Azure Fluid Relay; Microsoft documents a 700 KB per-op ceiling and 100 users per session but no ops-per-second figure, so a 30 Hz stream from every participant needs a test before we trust it. Live Share is a good fit for the low-rate state either way: who picked which avatar, scene changes, game events.

C1 · Virtual camera into Teams Demo fallback

Our page renders the avatar view, OBS captures it as a browser source and exposes a virtual camera, Teams picks it as the webcam. No Teams integration at all, so it is the path that cannot fail on demo day. OBS adds at least one frame; users report about 300 ms when its buffering is on, so buffering must be off and T5 measures the result. Every VTuber pipeline works this way, which is also the weakness: it is a presenter trick, not a product feature.

C2 · Teams video-effect app Experiment

The Teams JavaScript SDK has a videoEffects module: a sideloaded app registers a frame handler and the Teams client hands it every local camera frame as a WebCodecs VideoFrame (or an NV12 buffer on older hosts) and puts back whatever the app returns. That is the shortest possible path inside Teams: our tracker and avatar renderer run in the Teams client's own video pipeline, before encoding, and the avatar shows up in the normal video tile with no virtual camera. Microsoft requires at least 22 fps at 720p, so the whole tracker-plus-render has to fit in roughly 45 ms per frame, and the client will flag the app as frozen if it repeatedly fails.

Caveats to state plainly: the API is marked beta and "do not use in production", the docs do not list which clients support it (the source checks a mediaStream or sharedFrame runtime capability, which maps to new Teams desktop vs the classic Electron client), and it needs sideloading in a tenant. Microsoft ships a local Electron test tool with the sample repo, so T4 can measure our per-frame budget without any tenant.

C3 · Our page as a meeting participant, via Azure Communication Services Experiment

The ACS Calling SDK for JavaScript can join a Teams for Work meeting as an external participant, send a custom outgoing video stream built from canvas.captureStream(), and expose every remote participant's video and audio as a MediaStream. That turns the hopeOS page into a Teams endpoint: the rendered scene goes out as our video, and the Teams tiles of the people who are not tracked can be placed inside the 3D room. The tenant has to allow anonymous or external join, Teams for Home and end-to-end-encrypted meetings are excluded, and it is metered per participant-minute. Highest ceiling of the three return paths, and the most convincing "this is a Teams client" story, but it needs an Azure resource and the pilot tenant's policy before we can test it.

D · Meeting app on the stage with Live Share Core container

A Teams meeting app declares the meetingSidePanel and meetingStage contexts plus the MeetingStage.Write.Chat permission; a presenter shares it and every participant's client loads the same URL, so each person runs the scene locally and Live Share keeps them in sync. This is the right container for "everyone in the same room" and it is how Teams expects collaborative apps to work.

One documented limit matters to us. Microsoft states that device permissions are not supported in the pop-out chat window, tabs, and the meeting side panel. The meeting stage is not named either way, so T6 has to check whether getUserMedia works on the stage in new Teams desktop and in the browser client. If it does not, the tracker runs in a separate browser window (or as the C2 video-effect app) and the stage app is purely the shared renderer, which still works because the landmark relay decouples the two.

Recommended prototype

Build B first, on this machine and one more laptop, with the latency HUD on screen. Add D when we have a tenant to sideload into. Keep C1 ready as the demo-day return path and run C2 and C3 as time-boxed experiments to find out which one the pilot should bet on.

The packet

One participant, one packet per tracked frame. Everything the avatar rigs already consume, quantized to 16-bit and sent unreliably; a lost packet is replaced by the next one 33 ms later.

header   : userId (4 B) · seq (2 B) · captureTs ms (4 B) · flags (1 B)
pose     : 33 × (x,y,z) int16, normalized      198 B
hands    : 2 × 21 × (x,y,z) int16              252 B
face     : 52 blendshapes uint8 + head quat     56 B + 8 B
extras   : avatarId, game-object grabs, voice-cmd tokens (rare, separate reliable channel)
≈ 520–600 B → ~18 KB/s per person at 30 Hz

Remote avatars are drawn at now − 2 frames and interpolated between the last two packets; a missing third packet extrapolates for up to 100 ms and then holds. Capture timestamps plus a ping-based clock offset give a per-avatar "age" number for the HUD, which is what we point at during the demo.

The one real refactor

Everything else in this plan is a new file beside existing code. The exception is embodiment: today exactly one avatar syncs to the camera and every other avatar in the scene is an NPC, and the multi-person tracker collapses to one primary player before it reaches the rigs. The shared-room demo needs the opposite: each tracked person on this camera and each remote packet owns an avatar. The avatar registry already holds many rigs, so the lift is to make "who drives this rig" a per-avatar source (local player id, remote user id, or NPC mind) instead of a global slot. Do it as part of T2, before any Teams work, because every return path renders from that scene.

What Teams contributes without us building anything

  • Identity. A meeting app gets the signed-in user from the Teams SDK context, so "which avatar is Hannah" is answered by SSO, not by face recognition.
  • Audio. The meeting carries voice; we do not touch it. Voice commands keep using the browser microphone in our page, which is a separate capture from the Teams call.
  • Roster and roles. Presenter and organizer roles control who can share the scene to the stage; attendees cannot.

Local face identification

Because Teams already knows who is signed in, face ID only earns its place in three situations: more than one person in front of one camera (a huddle room, a shop floor), a shared screen or kiosk where nobody is signed in, and keeping avatar identity stable when two tracked people cross in frame. Those are exactly the "actual place they work at" scenarios, so it is worth prototyping, but as a scoped feature with consent built in.

  • Pipeline. Our face landmarker already finds each face; add an embedding model on a crop, once per second per track rather than per frame, and match by cosine distance against an enrolled set. Two browser-ready candidates: facex (Apache-2.0, WebAssembly, reports about 25 ms for detect plus embed on a desktop CPU, weights shipped encrypted) and an ArcFace or MobileFaceNet ONNX model through onnxruntime-web on WebGPU, which is the same runtime we use for sign recognition. Check model licences: several InsightFace weights are research-only.
  • Storage. Enrollment keeps five to ten embeddings per person, never frames, in IndexedDB on the device or encrypted in the org's own store. Matching is local; nothing biometric leaves the browser.
  • Consent pattern. Copy the one Microsoft already sells: Teams Rooms Pro "people recognition" requires each user to enrol a face profile in their own Teams settings, lets them unenrol at any time, and lets admins switch enrollment off by policy. Framing ours the same way removes most of the objections before they are raised. Biometric identifiers are special-category data under GDPR and regulated under Illinois BIPA, so opt-in enrollment and on-device processing are requirements, not niceties.
  • Multi-avatar in one browser. The pose, hand and face landmarkers all accept a count parameter; running them with two or three targets on one camera is the cheap way to demo two colleagues sharing a room camera, each driving their own avatar. T8 checks frame rate on this box with two people in frame.

Mini tests to run before building

Ordered so that the first five need nothing from Microsoft: this laptop, one more laptop, the existing dev server, and OBS. T6 and T7 wait on a tenant. Each test has one number that decides it.

  1. Latency HUD and the side-by-side lag probe

    Add per-frame timing to the existing tracking page: capture timestamp (from the video frame or requestVideoFrameCallback), tracker done, avatar posed, frame presented. Then open the same camera in Teams (or any second window), screen-capture that window with getDisplayMedia, run a second tracker on it, and drive a second avatar side by side. The lag is measured automatically by cross-correlating the wrist-height signal from the two trackers; no LED, no phone camera needed.

    builds on: the existing screen lane (srcActivate 'screen') and the HUD/EMA metrics in mpbrowser.html; sdk/core/tracking.js binds to one video element, so a second instance on a second element is the new part · ~1 day

    Decides: the measured gap between lanes A and B on our hardware. This is the demo slide.

  2. Landmark relay over WebSocket, two windows, then two laptops

    A 40-line Node relay (tools/relay.mjs, plain ws) that fans packets out to everyone else in a room. Two browser windows on this machine first, then this laptop plus one more on the same Wi-Fi. Implement the packet above, a ping-based clock offset, and 2-frame interpolation on the receiving side; the HUD shows each remote avatar's age.

    builds on: the BroadcastChannel presence bus and packet (mpbrowser.html ≈4278), the echoed-capture-timestamp RTT code (≈4230), and the sync design doc · new: tools/relay.mjs, sdk/net/landmark-packet.js, sdk/net/remote-avatar.js · plus the single-embodiment cap lift so a remote packet can own an avatar · ~3 days

    Decides: remote-avatar age under 80 ms on LAN at 30 Hz with two senders, under 25 KB/s per sender.

  3. Same test on WebRTC data channels and on Live Share's local host

    Swap the transport behind the same packet interface: an unordered, unreliable RTCDataChannel between the two laptops (signal through the same Node server), then Live Share's TestLiveShareHost running on its bundled tinylicious server, sending packets as LiveEvent values. Compare age and jitter at 30 Hz with two, then four, simulated senders.

    new: sdk/net/transport-datachannel.js, transport-liveshare.js · ~2 days

    Decides: which transport carries pose, and whether Live Share is limited to low-rate state.

  4. Video-effect frame budget in Microsoft's local test tool

    Clone microsoft/teams-videoapp-sample, unzip its Electron test app, and point it at a page of ours served over HTTPS on localhost. The page registers videoEffects.registerForVideoFrame, runs our face and hand trackers on the incoming VideoFrame, renders the avatar to a canvas, and returns it as a new frame. The tool reports average and range of processing time per frame at 720p and lets us halve or double the resolution.

    new: teamsfx/video-effect.html (tracker + avatar in one frame handler) · needs an HTTPS localhost server; the dev server is HTTP only · ~2 days · the test app is x64 Electron, so run it on the second laptop if it will not launch on this ARM64 box

    Decides: whether tracker plus render fits under 45 ms per frame at 720p. If yes, C2 is the return path to pitch.

  5. Virtual camera round trip

    OBS browser source pointed at the scene page, virtual camera on, buffering off, Teams set to use it. Measure the added lag with the T1 cross-correlation probe: direct tracker versus a tracker on Teams' self-view of the virtual camera.

    no code; OBS on the second laptop · ~half a day

    Decides: added lag under 100 ms means C1 is an acceptable demo fallback.

  6. Teams sideload probe (needs a tenant with sideloading)

    A minimal manifest: devicePermissions: ["media"], contexts meetingSidePanel and meetingStage, RSC permissions for the stage and Live Share, served through a Dev Tunnel or ngrok. In new Teams desktop and in the browser client, check four things: does getUserMedia work in a personal tab, in the side panel, and on the shared stage; does our WebSocket and a data channel connect from inside the app iframe; does the tracker keep 30 fps inside the Teams WebView; does a LiveEvent at 30 Hz from three clients arrive intact.

    new: teamsfx/manifest.json, teamsfx/stage.html (scene + Live Share join) · ~2 days once a tenant exists

    Decides: whether the tracker can live inside the stage app or must run in a separate window.

  7. Join a Teams meeting from our page through Azure Communication Services

    An ACS resource, a token endpoint (fits the existing Vercel serverless key-proxy pattern), and the Calling SDK. Join by meeting link, send canvas.captureStream(30) of the avatar view as our video, render one remote participant's MediaStream onto a plane in the scene.

    new: api/acs-token.js, teamsfx/acs-client.html · needs the pilot tenant to allow external join · ~2 days

    Decides: whether "hopeOS is a Teams participant" is viable for the pilot without any Teams app approval.

  8. Local face ID with two people in frame

    Run the face landmarker with two faces, add an embedding model on crops at 1 Hz per track, enrol two people (five embeddings each) into IndexedDB, and show name labels that survive the two people swapping places. Measure tracker frame rate with two people and embedding time per crop on this box.

    new: sdk/recognition/face-id.js, faceidlab.html · ~2 days

    Decides: whether the whole thing stays above 24 fps on a laptop with two people, and which embedding model we can license.

Questions to put to the Teams side

  • Can they give us a developer tenant (or enable sideloading in a sandbox tenant) so T6 can run? Without it, D and C2 cannot be tested at all.
  • Is the third-party video-effect (video filter) app path still open for pilots in new Teams, and on which clients (Windows, Mac, web)? The API is documented as beta; we need their read on its future.
  • Will the pilot tenant allow external participants to join meetings through Azure Communication Services? That single policy decides whether C3 is on the table.
  • Which clients do their target users run: new Teams desktop on Windows, Mac, or the browser? Camera access and the video-effect API differ by client.
  • Do the pilot locations have Teams Rooms with intelligent cameras? The "shared room camera, several avatars" scenario is much stronger where they already exist.

Notes for the business case (for later)

Not the topic of this memo, but three facts from the research are worth keeping for that conversation.

  • Microsoft retired the small-group 3D space. Mesh, the Mesh apps for PC and Quest, and the "Immersive space (3D)" view in Teams meetings were switched off in early December 2025 and replaced by "immersive events", which target large scheduled events and need Teams Premium to host. A tracked, in-meeting, browser-only avatar layer for small teams is a gap they just created themselves.
  • Avatars for Teams still exist but are not tracked. They are desktop-only, driven by audio and chosen emotes, and view-only on mobile. Camera-driven bodies and hands in a shared scene is a visible step up, not a duplicate.
  • Face recognition already ships in Teams Rooms Pro. Enrolled face and voice profiles, admin policy, per-user unenrol. Our local version follows an established pattern the buyer already accepted, which shortens the privacy conversation.
